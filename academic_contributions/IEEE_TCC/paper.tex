\begin{abstract}
  Kubernetes autoscalers must preserve service-level objectives while workloads, failures, and adversarial traffic perturb the same shared resources. Threshold-based Horizontal Pod Autoscaling (HPA) reacts to local metrics, whereas most reinforcement learning autoscalers optimize a monolithic objective and therefore struggle to coordinate responses to bottlenecks, pod failures, resource contention, and DDoS-like overloads. We study resilient Kubernetes autoscaling as an organizationally constrained multi-agent reinforcement learning problem and implement the approach in KARMA. The framework decomposes operational resilience into role-specific missions, constrains role-irrelevant actions through organizational roles, shapes local rewards through mission progress, and trains policies in a digital twin with MAPPO. This structure lets operators inspect, bound, and audit autoscaling decisions by operational responsibility instead of treating the learned controller as a single opaque policy. KARMA then analyzes learned behaviors and supports guarded transfer to Kubernetes through runtime guardrails. Evaluation on a chained-service Kubernetes topology compares KARMA with HPA and RL-based autoscalers, and includes ablations, disturbance-intensity studies, sim-to-real analysis, behavioral-separability metrics, and computational overhead measurements.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Cloud-native applications increasingly rely on Kubernetes to orchestrate microservices whose performance depends on shared compute, memory, network, and scheduling resources~\cite{Pahl2019}. Horizontal Pod Autoscaling (HPA) is the standard mechanism for changing the number of pod replicas according to observed load or resource utilization~\cite{Toka2020}. Choosing an adequate autoscaling policy is therefore operationally important: under-scaling violates service-level objectives (SLOs), over-scaling increases cost and contention, and delayed or poorly targeted scale-out can propagate congestion across dependent services~\cite{Santos2025Gwydion}. In practice, operational resilience is not captured by a single metric: high success rate, low latency, availability, bounded failure rate, and controlled resource consumption must be maintained simultaneously. These objectives can conflict, especially when a cluster experiences bottlenecks, cascading pod crashes, resource contention, or Distributed Denial-of-Service (DDoS)-like traffic surges~\cite{David2021,burns2016borg}.

The operational setting has also become more complex than the autoscaling assumptions embedded in many early cloud controllers. Microservice applications are often deployed behind service meshes, ingress controllers, monitoring sidecars, and distributed tracing stacks. A replica change can therefore modify not only the capacity of one deployment, but also request routing, queueing delays, placement decisions, and resource contention at neighboring services. In such environments, elasticity is not merely a performance optimization problem; it becomes a resilience problem in which the controller must preserve acceptable service behavior while the system is being perturbed.

Conventional Kubernetes HPA is reactive and metric-threshold based. It is effective for stable workload shifts but less suited to partially observable, adversarial, and multi-objective situations in which a local scaling action can move contention to another service or amplify an attacker-induced overload. Reinforcement learning (RL) autoscalers improve adaptability by learning policies from interactions~\cite{Gari2021,Mishra2024CloudRL}, but many existing approaches use a single decision maker and a global reward. A single agent must then learn all failure-specific responses, infer which service caused a degradation, arbitrate between incompatible objectives, and avoid unauthorized or role-irrelevant exploration.

This distinction matters for resilient autoscaling. Under benign workload variation, a controller can often optimize average latency or utilization. Under faults and adversarial traffic, the same controller must reason over several observed signals: queue growth may indicate a bottleneck, a DDoS-like surge, or an upstream failure; latency may improve by adding replicas or worsen because new replicas increase contention; and aggressive scale-out may protect availability while violating resource budgets. These interactions make the problem multi-objective, partially observable, and partially adversarial.

Multi-agent systems (MASs) provide a natural decomposition: specialized agents can focus on complementary operational missions while coordinating through shared state and reward signals~\cite{Shoham2009MAS,Jennings1998}. Yet an unconstrained multi-agent formulation is not sufficient. Without structure, agents may learn redundant or conflicting actions, converge slowly, or produce policies that are difficult for operators to interpret~\cite{guidotti2018survey,garcia2015safe}. Our Kubernetes Autoscaling with Resilient Multi-Agent system (KARMA) addresses this gap by introducing organizational specifications into multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL)~\cite{Yu2022,soule2025moisemarl}: roles restrict what agents are authorized to do, and missions shape why they act.

KARMA is positioned between three explicit families of autoscalers: HPA-style controllers, RL-based autoscalers, and MARL or organizational MARL controllers. It keeps the operational compatibility of HPA-style controllers by acting through Kubernetes replica decisions, adopts the adaptivity of RL autoscalers by learning from traces, and uses MARL to distribute control across degradation-specific agents. A preliminary version of this work appeared in IEEE CLOUD 2025~\cite{Soulée2025resilient}. The main contribution lies in the organizational layer: roles and missions are not post-hoc labels assigned after learning, but constraints and rewards that shape the learning process itself.

The main contributions are as follows: (1) we formalize resilient Kubernetes autoscaling as an organizationally-guided stochastic game in which operational resilience is decomposed into role-specific missions; (2) we introduce a role-guided action-constrained MARL formulation that reduces unauthorized and role-irrelevant exploration; (3) we define mission-guided reward shaping to align local agent objectives with global operational resilience. We implement these mechanisms in KARMA, an automated framework coupling digital-twin modeling, Multi-Agent Proximal Policy Optimization (MAPPO) training~\cite{Yu2022}, behavioral analysis, and Kubernetes deployment, and provide a comprehensive evaluation including ablation studies, robustness under varying attack and failure intensities, sim-to-real gap analysis, behavioral-separability metrics, and computational overhead.

The remainder of the paper positions KARMA against autoscaling and organizational-MARL work, introduces the formulation and framework, describes the evaluation protocol, reports results, and discusses deployment limits.

\autoref{tab:autoscaling_criteria} uses \emph{adversarial} for support of attack-like workload disturbances, \emph{multi-goal} for simultaneous optimization of several operational objectives, \emph{simulation} for explicit pre-deployment training or testing environments, and \emph{guardrails} for deployment-time constraints such as action bounds or fallback policies. The main point is that recent autoscalers often combine learning and real-cluster evaluation, but rarely combine adversarial resilience, multi-agent decomposition, behavior analysis, and deployment guardrails.

\textbf{Traditional autoscaling.} Kubernetes HPA adjusts replicas from metrics such as central processing unit (CPU) or memory utilization~\cite{Carrion2022}. The controller is simple, robust, and widely deployed, which explains its role as a practical fallback in KARMA. Its limitations are also well known: threshold choice is workload dependent, reactions are delayed by metric windows and stabilization periods, and single-metric control cannot directly encode availability, latency, and failure-recovery trade-offs. Adaptive variants extend the signal set or combine horizontal and vertical decisions~\cite{Zhou2024,KOSMOS,COPA}. These systems improve operational coverage but remain primarily reactive and do not model adversarial or cascading disturbances explicitly.

\textbf{RL-based autoscaling.} RL methods learn scaling policies from simulated or production traces. Gym-HPA provides an experimentation environment~\cite{gymhpa2022}; Rlad-core studies horizontal and vertical scaling with learning-based controllers~\cite{Rossi2019}; Automate Workload Autoscaling with Reinforcement Learning in production systems (AWARE) optimizes workload autoscaling in production cloud systems~\cite{aware2023}; quality-of-service-aware RL approaches target service-level objectives~\cite{QoSRL}. More recent work also studies microservice dependencies in Kubernetes through Gwydion~\cite{Santos2025Gwydion}, cloud-workload autoscaling with RL~\cite{Mishra2024CloudRL}, and automated online MAS design for resilient Kubernetes autoscaling~\cite{Soulée2025resilient}. These works improve adaptivity, but most use a single controller or a single global reward. That design can be effective for workload tracking, yet it gives little structure to fault-specific responsibilities and makes it difficult to inspect which learned behavior corresponds to which operational objective.

\textbf{MARL for cloud management.} MARL is appropriate when several decision makers affect a shared environment~\cite{Shoham2009MAS}. MAPPO has shown strong empirical performance in cooperative multi-agent settings through centralized training and decentralized execution~\cite{Yu2022}. Cloud management problems naturally expose shared-resource coupling: scaling one service can change the feasible actions of another, and improving one metric may degrade another. Recent Kubernetes-oriented MARL work such as the Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning-based In-place Scaling Engine (MARLISE) explores decentralized in-place resource scaling for edge-cloud systems~\cite{Prodanov2025MARLISE}. Existing MARL formulations often rely on centralized critics, shared rewards, or communication policies, but the operational meaning of each agent is usually implicit. KARMA differs by tying each agent to a role and mission before training.

\textbf{Organizational MAS.} Organizational models in MAS define roles, goals, permissions, and relationships between agents~\cite{Jennings1998,hubner2002moise,Boissier2007OOP}. They have been used to reason about coordination and accountability, and recent engineering approaches such as the Assisted MAS Organization Engineering Approach (AOMEA) support organization-oriented design for adaptive systems~\cite{soule2024aomea}. KARMA brings this idea into MARL for Kubernetes autoscaling: the organization does not replace learning, but restricts and shapes it. This creates an intermediate point between hand-coded operational playbooks and unconstrained neural controllers.

Recent work on organizationally oriented MARL argues that roles and missions can support controllability and explainability by making learned behavior inspectable at the level of responsibilities rather than only through network weights or action probabilities~\cite{soule2025moisemarl}. KARMA adopts this perspective in a cloud-control setting. The role guide constrains which operational interventions can be selected, while the mission guide exposes why a class of interventions is rewarded. This differs from post-hoc explanation because the organizational model changes the training distribution itself: agents encounter fewer irrelevant actions, and trajectory analysis can be checked against explicit role expectations.

\textbf{Explainable and safe RL.} Digital twins provide a controlled environment for testing cyber-physical and cloud control policies before deployment~\cite{fuller2020digital}, while explainability methods can expose behavioral regularities in otherwise opaque models~\cite{guidotti2018survey,biran2017explanation}. Safe reinforcement learning and runtime assurance separate policy optimization from deployment constraints~\cite{garcia2015safe}; KARMA follows that separation through trace-driven transition modeling, action masks, mission rewards, trajectory clustering, and runtime guardrails. Its contribution is therefore not only a framework integration but a method for organizing MARL exploration and deployment around operational resilience. We use Kubernetes HPA (KHPA), inherited from the Kubernetes autoscaling mechanism, as the default rule-based baseline~\cite{Toka2020}.

\section{Organizationally-guided MARL for resilient autoscaling}
\label{sec:org_marl}

The formulation starts from a global operational-resilience objective because operators ultimately care about service behavior, not about a single internal metric. KARMA then decomposes this objective into roles and missions because the same symptom can require different responses depending on its cause: latency under a bottleneck, latency under DDoS, and latency under resource contention are not operationally equivalent. The purpose of the formal model is therefore to preserve a shared resilience target while giving each agent a narrower responsibility and a more interpretable action.

\subsection{Problem formulation}
\label{subsec:problem_formulation}

We model resilient autoscaling as a stochastic game
\[
  \mathcal{SG}=\langle N,S,\{A_i\}_{i\in N},P,\{R_i\}_{i\in N},\gamma\rangle
\]
where $N=N_d\cup N_a$ contains defender autoscaling agents and, when modeled, an attacker agent; $S$ is the cluster state; $A_i$ is the action space of agent $i$; $P(s'|s,a_1,\ldots,a_n)$ is the real Kubernetes transition distribution; $R_i$ is the reward of agent $i$; and $\gamma\in[0,1]$ is the discount factor. Defender actions change deployment replicas,
\[
  a_i=(service\_id,\Delta replicas), \quad \Delta replicas\in[-\alpha,\alpha]
\]
whereas attacker perturb exposed traffic intensity or data.

The state $s\in S$ aggregates Prometheus~\cite{prometheus} and Kubernetes metrics for each deployment, including deployed and desired replicas, failed pods, pending requests, CPU and memory usage, ingress traffic, and egress traffic. Each agent observes $o_i$, a role-specific projection of $s$ and recent local actions. We denote by $a_d$ the joint defender action, by $a_a$ the attacker action, and by $R_{global}$ the operational-resilience reward shared by defenders. KARMA learns policies in a digital twin with dynamics $\hat{P}_\theta$ estimated from traces, then transfers the learned defender policies to the real cluster governed by $P$.

\subsection{Operational resilience decomposition}
\label{subsec:resilience_decomposition}

Operational resilience is defined as a normalized weighted objective:
\begin{align}
  \orfunc(s)= & w_{sr}SR(s)+w_{av}AV(s)+w_{lat}(1-LR(s)) \nonumber \\
              & +w_{fail}(1-FR(s))+w_{cap}TC(s),
  \label{eq:or}
\end{align}
where $SR$ is the ratio of successful requests to total requests, $AV$ is the fraction of available entry points, $LR$ is measured latency divided by the service-level latency bound and clipped to $[0,1]$, $FR$ is failed pods over deployed pods, and $TC$ is outgoing traffic over expected traffic clipped to $[0,1]$. Unless otherwise stated, the weights are set uniformly to avoid privileging one resilience component in the main comparison; sensitivity to these weights is left to the supplementary material. The defender reward and attacker reward are:
\begin{equation}
  R_d(s,a_d,a_a)=\orfunc(s), \qquad
  R_a(s,a_d,a_a)=-R_d(s,a_d,a_a)
  \label{eq:defender_attacker_rewards}
\end{equation}

KARMA decomposes this global objective into operational missions: bottleneck mitigation, DDoS response, failure recovery, and resource contention management. Each mission captures a recurring degradation mode and assigns a narrower learning signal to a specialized agent. The zero-sum reward relation in \autoref{eq:defender_attacker_rewards} models adversarial pressure during training and evaluation only; deployment applies defender policies through guardrails without exposing an interface.

\autoref{tab:or_components} clarifies how the aggregate objective is grounded in measurements. The components are deliberately normalized before aggregation so that a high-variance metric, such as bursty traffic, does not dominate the reward by scale alone. Latency and resource consumption are the most difficult components to optimize jointly, because scale-out can improve response time under bottlenecks but worsen contention when the shared node is already saturated. This normalization also makes the objective easier to adapt by changing the weights of the organizational priorities.

\subsection{Role-guided action constraints}
\label{subsec:role_constraints}

A role is represented as a role action guide $\rag_i$, which maps the current state or local observation history to an authorized action set:
\[
  A_i^{role}(s)=\rag_i(s)\subseteq A_i
\]
For hard organizational action masks, actions outside the role are masked:
\begin{equation}
  \pi_i(a|o_i)=0,\quad \forall a\notin A_i^{role}(s)
  \label{eq:hard_mask}
\end{equation}
For soft organizational reward shaping, the action remains available but receives a compliance bonus or violation penalty in the shaped reward.

If a role guide restricts each available action set from $A_i$ to $A_i^{role}(s)$, then the number of candidate joint defender actions is reduced from $\prod_{i\in N_d}|A_i|$ to $\prod_{i\in N_d}|A_i^{role}(s)|$. \textit{Interpretation.} The proposition follows directly from the Cartesian product of individual action sets. This reduction removes actions that are incoherent with an agent's operational responsibility, constrains exploration, and can accelerate convergence when the role is relevant regarding the cluster.

\subsection{Mission-guided reward shaping}
\label{subsec:mission_rewards}

A mission is encoded as a goal reward guide $\grg_i$ that adds local operational feedback to the global resilience reward:
\begin{equation}
  R_i'(s,a,s')=R_{global}(s,a,s')+\kappa_i R_{mission_i}(s,a,s')
  \label{eq:mission_reward}
\end{equation}
where $\kappa_i$ is the mission-shaping coefficient of agent $i$.
Then, KARMA adds a compliance term for a  mission:
\[
  R_i'=R_i+\beta\,bonus_{compliance}-\eta\,penalty_{violation}
\]
The mission-shaped reward in \autoref{eq:mission_reward} and the compliance shaping term above serve different purposes: missions encode operational progress, while compliance bonuses and penalties encode whether an action respects the organizational guide. For hard organizational action masks, invalid actions are masked by (\autoref{eq:hard_mask}) and do not enter the policy distribution. Soft organizational reward shaping preserves exploration but may delay stabilization; hard masks constrain deployment-relevant choices more strongly at the cost of relying on accurate role definitions.

\subsection{Integration with MAPPO}
\label{subsec:mappo_integration}

KARMA integrates organizational specifications into MAPPO~\cite{Yu2022}. A centralized critic observes the global cluster state and estimates a shared value function, while decentralized actors execute role-constrained policies from local observations. The global reward maintains cooperation, role masks structure the action space, and mission shaping provides dense local feedback.
%
Algorithm~\ref{alg:org_mappo} shows where these organizational elements enter the MAPPO loop: roles constrain action sampling before exploration, missions modify the reward signal before policy updates, and exported policies are analyzed before guarded transfer into the Kubernetes control loop.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
  \caption{Organizationally-Guided MAPPO in KARMA}
  \label{alg:org_mappo}
  \scriptsize
  \begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \REQUIRE Digital twin $\hat{P}_\theta$, roles $\rag_i$, missions $\grg_i$, policies $\pi_i$, convergence thresholds $\mu,\rho$
    \REPEAT
    \STATE Collect trajectories in $\hat{P}$ from current policies.
    \FOR{each step and defender agent $i$}
    \STATE Compute $A_i^{role}(s)$ and apply the role action mask.
    \STATE Sample $a_i\sim\pi_i(\cdot|o_i)$ over authorized actions.
    \ENDFOR
    \STATE Compute $R_{global}$ from operational resilience.
    \STATE Add mission rewards and compliance bonuses/penalties.
    \STATE Update MAPPO actors and centralized critic.
    \STATE Estimate reward mean and standard deviation over episodes.
    \UNTIL{reward standard deviation $<\mu$ and cumulative reward $>\rho$}
    \STATE Export policies for analysis and guarded deployment.
  \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Design rationale}

KARMA uses roles because resilient autoscaling failures require different responses: bottlenecks, DDoS surges, pod crashes, and resource contention produce overlapping symptoms but demand distinct corrective actions. Assigning each agent a role narrows the action set to operationally meaningful decisions for each degradation mode, reducing learner burden and aligning learned policies with incident-response reasoning.

Missions complement roles by specifying the local objective attached to each responsibility. A role determines which actions are admissible; a mission determines which effects are desirable. This separation is useful because an agent may be authorized to scale a service in several states, but the reward should still distinguish whether that action reduces pending requests, shortens failure recovery, or protects a critical service from contention. The mission signal therefore provides denser feedback while preserving a shared global resilience objective.

MAPPO is used because the control problem requires centralized training and decentralized execution. During training, the critic can observe global cluster state and cross-service interactions. During deployment, each actor can act from local observations and role-specific masks. Organizational guidance is applied before and during learning instead of as post-processing: masking constrains exploration trajectories, and mission shaping changes the gradient signal. A post-hoc classifier over learned actions would leave unauthorized or role-irrelevant exploration in the training process.

\subsection{Role and mission engineering}

\autoref{fig:org_decomposition_workflow} summarizes how organizational specifications are engineered. The figure is a derivation workflow, not an outline of the surrounding subsections: it explains how the operational objective is progressively transformed into roles, missions, reward terms, and learning constraints. The process begins from the operational-resilience objective in (\autoref{eq:or}), then identifies degradation modes that repeatedly reduce one or more components of the objective. Each degradation mode is mapped to a role when it requires a distinct class of corrective actions. The bottleneck manager reacts to queue buildup and throughput imbalance; the DDoS manager reacts to ingress bursts and availability pressure; the failure manager reacts to pod unavailability; and the resource manager reacts to CPU and memory contention that affects critical services.

Missions are then derived from the resilience component most directly affected by the degradation mode. Bottleneck mitigation emphasizes pending requests and throughput recovery; DDoS response emphasizes availability and bounded latency; failure recovery emphasizes downtime; and resource contention management emphasizes protection of critical services under pressure. These missions are intentionally simple so that the organizational specification remains auditable. More complex reward terms can be added, but doing so risks making the organization as opaque as the learned policy.

The choice between hard organizational action masks and soft organizational reward shaping is made according to deployment risk. Hard masks are appropriate when an action is operationally invalid, such as scaling a service outside replica limits or assigning a failure-recovery agent to an unrelated deployment. Soft shaping is preferable when the role is a preference rather than a strict permission, for example when a contention manager may temporarily scale a non-critical service to prevent overload propagation. KARMA evaluates both choices in the ablation study.

This engineering process is intentionally conservative. Roles are not learned from scratch in the current implementation because the target deployment setting requires operators to understand why an agent is allowed to act on a given service. Learned roles are a promising future direction, but the present study focuses on whether explicit organizational structure can make MARL more stable and auditable for autoscaling.

\subsection{World Model-based Digital Twin Construction}

KARMA constructs a digital twin of the Kubernetes cluster from Prometheus metrics and Kubernetes events. Instead of relying on a handcrafted simulator, it learns a \emph{World Model}~\cite{ha2018world,planet2019,dreamer2020,mbpo2019} that approximates the environment dynamics, enabling offline reinforcement learning before deployment.

Execution traces are converted into a transition dataset
$\mathcal{D}=\{(s_t,a_t,s_{t+1})\}$,
where $s_t$ denotes the monitored cluster state and $a_t$ the autoscaling action. The learned transition model $\hat{P}_{\theta}$ predicts the next state from observed transitions and generalizes to unseen situations. During training, this model replaces the real Kubernetes cluster to generate simulated trajectories for MAPPO, reducing interactions with the cluster.

Unlike handcrafted digital twins~\cite{fuller2020digital}, the learned model continuously captures the relationship between workload evolution, resource utilization, failures, and autoscaling decisions directly from operational data, allowing the digital twin to evolve with the monitored infrastructure.

The World Models approach assumes that environment monitoring provides an approximately Markovian state representation. Prediction errors may accumulate over long simulated rollouts (\emph{compounding error})~\cite{planet2019,dreamer2020}; their impact is evaluated through the sim-to-real analysis in \autoref{sec:sim_to_real}.

\subsection{Training pipeline}

The training pipeline initializes agents, roles, missions, action masks, and reward weights, then runs MAPPO inside the digital twin. Optuna~\cite{akiba2019optuna} tunes selected hyperparameters, including learning rate, entropy coefficient, clipping range, and organizational hardness. Convergence is accepted when cumulative reward exceeds a minimum threshold and reward variance remains below a threshold across evaluation episodes.

\subsection{Digital Twin validation and hyperparameter protocol}

The digital twin is validated separately from policy optimization. Transition traces are split into training and evaluation partitions by workload window rather than by random transition shuffling, so that evaluation transitions contain unseen temporal patterns. We track one-step prediction error for resource metrics and categorical accuracy for discrete failure states. The sim-to-real study in \autoref{sec:results} is then used as an end-to-end validation, since low one-step error does not guarantee accurate closed-loop behavior on long horizons.

Hyperparameter optimization is constrained to a fixed Optuna budget for KARMA and the learning-based baselines. The search space includes MAPPO learning rate, entropy coefficient, clipping range, and role-mask hardness; the digital-twin architecture is kept fixed in the main experiments to avoid conflating model-capacity tuning with organizational effects. Convergence uses two conditions: average reward must exceed a scenario-specific threshold, and reward standard deviation must remain below a stability threshold over recent evaluation episodes. Complete run configurations, selected trials, and trace schemas are kept in supplementary material to keep the main paper within the journal page budget.

\subsection{Explainability Pipeline}

KARMA analyzes trained agents before transfer. Role consistency is measured by clustering action trajectories with Dynamic Time Warping (DTW)~\cite{berndt1994using}. Mission consistency is measured by clustering visited states in successful trajectories. Interaction graphs summarize observed acquaintance, authority, and communication relations between agents over evaluation windows. We distinguish alignment score, which measures compliance with predefined roles and missions, from clustering purity, which measures whether observed behaviors form clean groups, and cluster stability, computed as average adjusted Rand index across training seeds. These metrics should be interpreted as behavioral-separability indicators, not as a substitute for an operator user study.

\subsection{Transfer and runtime guardrails}

During transfer, Prometheus metrics are converted into the current state, each policy proposes a replica action, and authorized actions are applied through the Kubernetes API. KARMA constrains deployment actions through:
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
    A_i^{safe}(s)=\{a\in A_i \mid\; & r_{min}\le r_i+\Delta(a)\le r_{max}, \\
                                    & cost(a)\le C_{max}\}
  \end{aligned}
  \label{eq:safe_actions}
\end{equation}
The deployed controller is:
\begin{equation}
  \pi_{deploy}(s)=
  \begin{cases}
    \pi_{KARMA}(s), & valid(s,a),       \\
    \pi_{\khpa}(s), & \text{otherwise}.
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:deploy_policy}
\end{equation}
where $r_{min}$ and $r_{max}$ are replica bounds, $cost(a)$ is the estimated resource increase induced by action $a$, $C_{max}$ is the per-decision resource budget, and $valid(s,a)$ means $a\in A_i^{safe}(s)$ from (\autoref{eq:safe_actions}) and $drift(s)\le\delta$. The drift score is the normalized distance between the current observation and the digital-twin validation distribution. In practice, action caps, drift detection, canary deployment, and KHPA fallback implement (\autoref{eq:deploy_policy}) as runtime guardrails that constrain deployment actions. The main paper evaluates the learned controller and reports the guardrail design; rejected-action counts, fallback frequency, and drift-triggered fallback events require full deployment logs and are left to the artifact.

\section{Experimental methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}

The evaluation is designed to test whether organizational guidance improves resilience for the reasons claimed in the formulation. Single-degradation scenarios isolate whether each role can react to its intended incident class, while the mixed scenario tests whether those roles remain useful when symptoms overlap and actions interfere. The methodology therefore separates environment design, workload perturbations, baselines, metrics, and reproducibility controls before reporting the aggregate comparison results in the next section.

\subsection{Kubernetes environment}

The evaluation uses a Chained Services Kubernetes topology with four interconnected services, shown in \autoref{fig:chained_services_graph}. The cluster runs on one worker node with 8 virtual CPUs, 32 gigabytes of memory, and 1 gigabit-per-second network bandwidth. Training and analysis run on a separate compute node with NVIDIA Tesla V100 accelerators, Intel Xeon processors, and 128 gigabytes of memory available for experiments.

The topology is intentionally small but operationally coupled. A four-service chained graph is representative of a simple microservice dependency path: requests pass through several deployments, and the behavior of one deployment affects downstream latency and upstream queueing. Four services are enough to expose propagation, contention, and non-local replica effects, because an action on one service can change both the queue observed upstream and the resource pressure observed by neighboring services. Local queue growth can therefore propagate as latency at a different service, and a replica decision at one deployment can change contention experienced by the others. This makes the environment more informative than an isolated single-service benchmark while keeping the causal structure inspectable. It also matches the organizational design: each role can be evaluated against a recognizable degradation mode without hiding the effect of shared resources and Kubernetes scheduling.

\subsection{Baselines and ablations}

External baselines are KHPA, Gym-HPA~\cite{gymhpa2022}, Rlad-core~\cite{Rossi2019}, and AWARE~\cite{aware2023}. Ablations isolate single-agent learning, multi-agent learning without organizational specifications, roles only, missions only, soft organization, hard organization, and full KARMA with hard organization, neural digital twin, analysis, and transfer guardrails. Learning-based baselines use the same episode budget unless their original implementation reaches its published stopping rule earlier.

Baseline configuration follows two principles. First, each controller receives the metrics required by its design, rather than an artificially restricted common subset that would disadvantage methods using richer observations. Second, the training and evaluation budgets are aligned at the protocol level: learning-based baselines use the same scenario definitions, decision intervals, trace partitions, and evaluation seeds. These principles reduce protocol-induced bias without forcing all controllers into the same observation model. This is important because the comparison should test control logic under comparable exposure, not whether a baseline can operate after removing the signals assumed by its original design.

\subsection{Metrics}

We evaluate operational resilience through success rate, latency compliance, pending requests, recovery time, service availability, reward variance, digital-twin gap, behavioral-separability scores, and computational cost. The results are organized around six research questions (RQs):
\begin{enumerate*}[label=\textbf{RQ\arabic*:}, itemjoin={;\quad }]
  \item Does KARMA improve operational resilience compared with existing HPA systems?
  \item What is the contribution of each KARMA component?
  \item How robust is KARMA under increasing attack/failure intensity?
  \item How large is the sim-to-real gap introduced by the digital twin?
  \item Do organizational specifications improve behavioral separability and support explainability?
  \item What is the computational overhead of KARMA?
\end{enumerate*}
Unless stated otherwise, learning-based results are averaged over ten independent seeds and reported with standard deviation for the main operational metrics. Since raw per-seed logs are not embedded in the manuscript, we do not infer $p$-values from aggregate statistics; the public artifact and supplementary material are intended to provide per-run data and scripts for confidence-interval and paired-test reproduction. Consequently, improvements should be interpreted from the reported effect sizes and variance until the accompanying per-run logs are used for statistical testing.

\subsection{Evaluation protocol and reproducibility}

Each training run uses a fixed workload scenario for policy optimization and a disjoint set of randomized workload traces for evaluation. Policies are evaluated over ten independent runs per scenario, with randomized disturbance start times and workload magnitudes within the scenario-specific ranges. The autoscaling decision interval is 30 s and Prometheus metrics are scraped every 5 s. The mixed scenario interleaves bottleneck, DDoS-like overload, pod-failure, and contention events to evaluate interference between missions.

\subsection{Cross-scenario interpretation}

The cross-scenario pattern is more informative than any single one. KARMA helps most when the same symptom has several plausible causes: high latency may call for scale-out under a bottleneck, restraint under resource contention, or availability protection under DDoS-like overload. Roles encode this distinction as a control prior, which explains why the largest gains appear when the controller must choose between competing explanations. In simpler cases, KARMA's advantage is more modest and mainly comes from avoiding irrelevant actions and reducing reward variance. The mixed scenario remains the most discriminating test because it exposes interference between missions; organizational guidance improves response discipline under ambiguity, but capacity limits and unmodeled effects still bound achievable resilience.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The discussion consolidates the RQs around mechanism, cost, deployment, and validity limits. Organizational guidance affects MARL through three mechanisms: role masks reduce the joint action space, missions provide denser local reward signals, and specialization reduces conflicts between agents that would otherwise scale the same services for incompatible reasons. This is most useful when different incidents produce overlapping metrics: bottlenecks, DDoS-like floods, pod crashes, and contention may all affect latency and availability, but they do not require identical corrective actions. The organization constrains the hypothesis space explored by MAPPO before post-hoc explanation is attempted, which supports controllability and behavioral inspection in the sense of organizationally guided MARL~\cite{soule2025moisemarl}.

KARMA trades role-design and training complexity for structured control. The present evaluation uses four services, four roles, a 30 s autoscaling interval, and inference below 30 ms, but larger multi-node clusters would add placement, network locality, and heterogeneous-node constraints. KARMA avoids an agent-per-service design by defining agents around degradation modes, yet larger deployments may still require hierarchical roles, service grouping, or learned role assignment. Roles also need maintenance: if they are too narrow, useful actions may be masked; if they are overfitted, they may authorize wrong services after cluster's changes.

Deployment should therefore be guarded and incremental rather than a direct replacement of Kubernetes HPA. Operators should train policies in the digital twin, inspect role alignment, use canary deployment, cap replica changes, and fallback to KHPA under drift or rejected actions. Monitoring fallback frequency, rejected actions, drift triggers, and service-priority changes indicates when roles or guardrails no longer match the operational setting. KARMA is most appropriate when incident classes recur and prolonged degradation is expensive; for stable services, a tuned HPA policy may remain preferable.

This also clarifies the intended operator interface. KARMA does not ask a site-reliability engineer to interpret raw neural-network parameters; it exposes role definitions, mission rewards, rejected actions, and trajectory-alignment metrics as reviewable artifacts. These artifacts are coarse compared with a full causal explanation, but they are close to the operational vocabulary used during incident response.

The same interface gives operators an intermediate intervention point between retraining and manual override. If a policy behaves correctly except for one unauthorized class of actions, the role mask can be tightened without discarding the learned controller. Conversely, if a soft mission is too conservative, its shaping weight can be adjusted and evaluated in the digital twin before deployment. This does not remove the need for retraining after large topology changes, but it gives teams a structured way to modify the controller's behavioral envelope while preserving auditability.

The remaining practical trade-off is organizational maintenance. A role specification should be treated like an operational policy artifact rather than as a one-time model parameter: it needs versioning, review after topology changes, and validation against incident traces. This maintenance cost is justified when recurring incidents make recovery time and behavioral auditability significantly important.

Scalability should also be interpreted at the level of responsibilities rather than only at the level of services. The four-service experiment is intentionally small, but the coupling between services is sufficient to expose propagation, contention, and non-local scaling effects. A larger deployment would not necessarily require one agent per service; it could group services by criticality, dependency layer, or incident class. This design keeps the organizational abstraction useful, but it also means that scaling KARMA to production requires role-engineering decisions that should be validated with operators and production-like traces.

This interpretation also bounds the claim made by the experiments. KARMA does not infer a complete causal model of the cluster, and it does not guarantee that every ambiguous incident will be assigned to the correct role. Its contribution is more operational: it narrows the set of plausible actions, gives each agent a responsibility that can be audited, and makes disagreement between expected and learned behavior easier to locate. The most appropriate use case is not a stable service with simple traffic patterns, but an application where incidents recur, recovery time is costly, and operators need a way to inspect why a learned autoscaler acted on a service.

The soft-organization variant is important in this context. Hard masks are useful when actions are clearly unsafe or outside an operational mandate, but they can hide useful recovery actions if the role specification is incomplete. Soft shaping preserves exploration while still biasing the policy toward the organizational model. In practice, a deployment could start with softer guidance during digital-twin evaluation and move only well-understood constraints into hard masks.

This staged path is also useful for audits because every promotion from soft guidance to hard masking can be justified by replayed traces and operator review.

Finally, the results should be read as evidence for response discipline rather than as evidence that KARMA discovers universally optimal scaling policies. The main comparison, ablations, robustness study, and behavioral-separability analysis all point to the same mechanism: organizational guidance is valuable when the controller must choose among several plausible interpretations of a shared metric pattern. The framework makes this choice more explicit by separating bottleneck, attack, failure, and contention responsibilities. It still depends on the quality of the role model, the representativeness of the digital twin, and the capacity envelope of the cluster. This is why the paper keeps KHPA fallback, action caps, drift detection, and operator review in the deployment path instead of presenting learned policies as unbounded autonomous control. In production, these guardrails should be treated as part of the controller, not as optional engineering details.

\subsection{Threats to validity}

\textbf{Internal and baseline validity.} Results may depend on random seeds, hyperparameters, failure injection scripts, role definitions, and baseline reimplementations. We align budgets, seeds, decision timing, and scenarios, but configuration choices can still affect comparative performance.

\textbf{External and workload validity.} The evaluation uses a single-node Chained Services topology with generated perturbations rather than long production traces. Larger multi-node clusters, heterogeneous nodes, diurnal patterns, flash crowds, retries, and correlated failures may expose additional scheduling and network effects.

\textbf{Construct, reward, and model validity.} Operational resilience is a weighted aggregation, and mission rewards encode design assumptions. Other organizations may prefer different SLO trade-offs. The neural digital twin also cannot perfectly capture Kubernetes scheduling, restart dynamics, network burstiness, or concurrent-disturbance interference, which explains the larger mixed-scenario sim-to-real gap.

\textbf{Role engineering validity.} KARMA relies on manually engineered roles and missions. These specifications improve interpretability and constrain exploration, but may encode assumptions that do not transfer unchanged. Future evaluations should include a thorough operator review.

\textbf{Security and operational validity.} The DDoS scenario emulates adversarial overload but not an adaptive attacker. Runtime guardrails are included in the design, but fallback frequency, rejected-action counts, and drift-trigger statistics are reported outside the main paper. Future security validation should use adaptive attack policies, production-like incident datasets, and explicit measurement of whether guardrails prevent unsafe scale-out.

\textbf{Measurement and reproducibility validity.} Metrics depend on Prometheus scrape intervals, request accounting, latency measurement points, Kubernetes version, container images, hardware, and network conditions. The artifact is therefore a reproducibility package for the protocol, not a guarantee of bit-level identical results; raw logs support further confidence-interval or paired-test analysis.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We studied Kubernetes autoscaling as an organizationally guided MARL problem and implemented the approach in KARMA. The framework decomposes operational resilience into role-specific missions, constrains role-irrelevant exploration, aligns local rewards with global resilience, trains policies in a digital twin, and transfers them through runtime guardrails. The practical implication is to treat organizational guidance as a deployment contract: it specifies which actions a learned autoscaler may take, which objectives justify them, and which behaviors operators can audit before rollout.

The results show that KARMA's benefit is not limited to aggregate baseline comparison. Ablations separate the effects of roles and missions, robustness experiments show slower degradation under increasing DDoS and pod-failure intensity, sim-to-real analysis exposes remaining digital-twin bias, behavioral-separability metrics support operator inspection, and cost measurements show the training and memory overhead of this structure. The main limitations are expert-defined roles, single-node evaluation, generated disturbances, and the mixed-scenario sim-to-real gap. Future work will evaluate larger multi-node clusters, refine roles automatically and through operator review, integrate production-like traces, and test adaptive adversarial scenarios with stronger validation.

These results should therefore be read as evidence for an engineering pattern rather than as a claim that one fixed policy covers all cloud applications. Organizational guidance is most useful when it is maintained with the same care as deployment manifests, SLO definitions, and incident-response rules. Its value depends on keeping roles aligned with operational authority, missions aligned with measurable resilience objectives, and guardrails aligned with the risk tolerance of the cluster.
